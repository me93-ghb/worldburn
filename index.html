what is this?

A live globe of every fire the satellites can currently see. It's not a science tool. NASA's own FIRMS fire map does that job properly, and if you want rigor, go there. This page just makes the amount of fire on Earth visible in one look.

where the data comes from

Fire detections come from NASA FIRMS (the MODIS instrument, rolling 7 days, refreshed about every half hour).

Coastlines and borders are Natural Earth, via world-atlas.

What one dot means: the satellite reads the ground in squares about a kilometre across and flags a square when something inside it burns hot enough to register, which takes roughly 1,000 m² of active flame, or as little as 100 m² in clear conditions. So a dot is not a burning city. It is a patch somewhere between a large garden and a square kilometre. Campfires are far too small to appear. A big wildfire is never one dot, it is a cluster of dozens or hundreds, which is why the serious fires read as blotches rather than points. On this globe the dots are drawn much larger than a kilometre, or you would not see them at all.

Satellites measure where a fire is, how hot it burns and for how long. They can't see who lit it. So the filter splits fires by behavior, which is measurable: fires that keep burning in the same place for days, and fires that flare up and are gone within a day. Offshore heat is presumed to be a gas flare, though a rig accident or a burning ship would look identical. The volcano markers are fixed locations of frequently active volcanoes, not live eruption reports.

We acknowledge the use of data from NASA's Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS), part of NASA's Earth Science Data and Information System.

things worth knowing

Fire makes the news when it threatens homes. Most of the world's fire never does. Roughly 70% of the area burned each year is in Africa, most of it savanna set alight on purpose by farmers and herders the way it has been for thousands of years. The burning follows the dry season across the continent. That's why the middle of Africa glows.

The belt moves with the seasons. North of the equator the burning runs October to March and peaks in December. South of the equator it runs April to September and peaks in July and August. The band slides across the continent with the dry season, so how alarming this globe looks depends on the month you open it.

About 774 million hectares burn worldwide in an average year, an area roughly the size of Australia, and Africa is around 63% of it. The global total is steadier than you would think, varying about 8% from year to year, and it has been falling: burned area dropped roughly a quarter between 1998 and 2015 as cropland expansion broke the savannas into smaller pieces (Andela et al. 2017).

Deliberate doesn't mean brief. A savanna front can hold the same spot for days, and about one in five of the belt's fires shows up under the persistent filter. In the boreal north it's four in five: up there, persistent almost always means a true wildfire.

The blue-white dots offshore never go out. They're gas flares, natural gas burned off as waste at oil platforms, day and night, all year. The World Bank counted 167 billion cubic meters flared in 2025, about twice what Germany uses in a year, burned for nothing. Collecting it would also stop the methane that slips through the flame unburned.

Most fire on Earth is set on purpose. The fires that make the news, the ones that rage for weeks, are the exception. The quiet majority is agriculture.

The total fire power number in the corner is a measurement, not a model. Satellites read the radiant heat of every fire they pass over. It usually sits between 600 and 900 GW, comfortably more than the combined electrical output of every nuclear plant on Earth, and that is only the heat the satellites see radiated, not the full energy of the burning.
